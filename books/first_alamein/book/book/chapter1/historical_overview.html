First Battle of El Alamein

Historical Overview: First Battle of El Alamein

Battle Narrative

July 1-3, 1942 - Rommel's Opening Assault and Deir el Shein

Rommel launched his offensive on July 1, 1942, just days after the fall of Tobruk. His plan called for 90th Light Division and Italian XX Corps to attack the southern sector while Afrika Korps exploited northward to the coast, encircling British forces.

The opening attack struck the Indian 18th Brigade box at Deir el Shein. German 90th Light Division, supported by 88mm guns and captured Grant tanks, assaulted the fortified position. The Indian garrison—approximately 4,000 men with 25-pounder guns and 2-pounder anti-tank guns—fought with desperate courage.

For 24 crucial hours, the Indians held. Artillery duel at point-blank range, infantry assaults repulsed, counterattacks launched. By evening July 1, the box was overrun and the garrison virtually destroyed—but the 24-hour delay was decisive. It allowed Auchinleck to organize defenses and move reserves into blocking positions.

The delay at Deir el Shein bought time for New Zealand and Indian forces to establish defensive positions on Ruweisat Ridge—key terrain dominating the central sector. German attempts to advance (July 2-3) ran into stiffening resistance. The momentum of Rommel's pursuit had been broken.

July 10-12, 1942 - Australian Attacks at Tel el Eisa

With Rommel's offensive stalled, Auchinleck ordered counter-attacks to keep Axis forces off-balance. The 9th Australian Division launched a dawn assault on July 10 against Axis positions at Tel el Eisa (Point 63) in the northern sector.

The Australians attacked with their characteristic aggression—infantry supported by Valentine tanks and preceded by artillery barrage. The attack caught German 164th Light Division and Italian Brescia Division unprepared. Australian infantry, experts at close-quarters combat, overwhelmed defensive positions in brutal fighting.

By July 11, Australians had created a significant salient at Tel el Eisa. German counterattacks attempted to restore the position but were repulsed with heavy losses. The Australian success demonstrated that properly coordinated combined-arms attacks could succeed—a marked contrast to earlier British failures.

July 14-16, 1942 - Battle of Ruweisat Ridge I

Auchinleck ordered major offensive operations to exploit Australian success. New Zealand 2nd Division, supported by armor and Indian troops, attacked Ruweisat Ridge on July 14-15.

The attack began as a night assault—New Zealand infantry infiltrated German positions under cover of darkness. Initial success was achieved, with infantry capturing portions of the ridge. However, supporting tanks failed to arrive on time, and when they did appear at dawn, lacked infantry support.

German dawn counter-attack with Panzer IIIs, Panzer IVs, and integrated anti-tank guns struck the exposed New Zealand infantry and unsupported British tanks separately. The attack failed with heavy casualties—demonstrating that even improving British tactics remained vulnerable to German combined-arms expertise.

Axis counterattacks recaptured most gains. The battle ended inconclusively but inflicted significant casualties on both sides.

July 21-22, 1942 - El Mreir Depression: The Tank Graveyard

The most catastrophic British failure came at El Mreir Depression on July 21-22. The newly-arrived 23rd Armoured Brigade (equipped with Valentine infantry tanks) was ordered to attack through the depression to support another Ruweisat Ridge assault.

The brigade advanced directly into a German killing ground. Concealed 88mm guns, PAK 40 75mm anti-tank guns, and Panzer IV F2 long 75mm tanks positioned on surrounding ridges opened fire. The slow, vulnerable Valentine tanks (maximum speed 15 mph, thin armor) were systematically destroyed.

In one of the worst British armor losses in a single day, over 80 Valentine tanks were destroyed—23rd Armoured Brigade was virtually annihilated. The "tank graveyard" of El Mreir demonstrated that British armor tactics, despite improvements, remained vulnerable to German integrated anti-tank defenses.

July 22-27, 1942 - Final Australian Attacks and Stalemate

Throughout late July, 9th Australian Division continued aggressive operations in the northern sector. Night attacks at Alam el Onsol (July 26-27) demonstrated Australian mastery of night operations—infiltrating German and Italian positions, overrunning defensive lines, and holding gains against dawn counterattacks.

The Australian attacks, though limited in scope, achieved disproportionate impact. They tied down German reserves, inflicted steady casualties, and demonstrated Allied capability for offensive action. The 9th Australian Division earned a reputation as one of the finest formations in Eighth Army.

By July 27, both sides were exhausted. Rommel's fuel and ammunition were nearly depleted—offensive operations were impossible. British attacks had also stalled with heavy losses. The First Battle of El Alamein ended in operational stalemate.

Aftermath: The Turning Point

Though the battle ended without decisive victory, strategically it marked the turning point of the desert war. Rommel's offensive had been permanently halted—never again would Axis forces threaten Cairo or the Nile Delta. The initiative had shifted to the Commonwealth.

Auchinleck was relieved of command in August (unfairly, many historians argue—his defensive masterpiece saved Egypt). General Bernard Montgomery arrived to command Eighth Army, inheriting Auchinleck's defensive success and preparing for the Second Battle of El Alamein (October 1942) that would decisively defeat Rommel.

Timeline

Phase 1: Rommel's Offensive Halted (July 1-5)

  • July 1: Rommel's offensive begins; assault on Deir el Shein
  • July 1, Evening: Deir el Shein overrun after 24-hour defense
  • July 2-3: German advances stall against New Zealand positions at Ruweisat Ridge
  • July 3-5: Inconclusive fighting; Rommel's momentum broken

Phase 2: British Counter-Offensives Begin (July 10-12)

  • July 10, Dawn: 9th Australian Division attacks Tel el Eisa
  • July 11: Australian success; Tel el Eisa salient created
  • July 12: German counterattacks repulsed

Phase 3: Ruweisat Ridge Battles (July 14-17)

  • July 14-15: New Zealand night attack on Ruweisat Ridge
  • July 15-16: German dawn counterattacks; second Ruweisat assault
  • July 16-17: Axis counterattacks recapture most gains; heavy casualties both sides

Phase 4: El Mreir Disaster (July 21-22)

  • July 21-22: 23rd Armoured Brigade advances into El Mreir Depression
  • July 22: Tank graveyard—80+ Valentine tanks destroyed in killing ground

Phase 5: Final Attacks and Stalemate (July 22-27)

  • July 22-25: Continued inconclusive fighting along the front
  • July 26-27: 9th Australian Division night attack at Alam el Onsol
  • July 27: Battle ends in exhaustion; operational stalemate

Outcome and Casualties

Tactical Result: Operational stalemate; strategic British victory

Casualties:

  • Commonwealth: 13,000 casualties (killed, wounded, captured); approximately 150 tanks destroyed
  • German: 7,000 casualties; approximately 40 tanks destroyed
  • Italian: 10,000 casualties (higher proportion due to Australian attacks on Italian sectors)

Tank Losses:

  • British: 150 tanks destroyed (including 80+ at El Mreir alone)
  • Axis: 40-50 tanks destroyed
  • However, Axis could not replace losses; British tanks arrived continuously from Egypt

Strategic Impact:

First El Alamein was the turning point of the North African campaign, though it appeared indecisive at the time. Rommel's offensive—which had carried Axis forces from El Agheila to El Alamein in just 7 months—was permanently halted. The strategic balance had shifted:

For the Axis:

  • Furthest advance achieved; never again threatened Cairo
  • Supply lines overextended and unsustainable
  • Tank and vehicle strength depleted beyond recovery
  • Fuel crisis chronic and worsening
  • Strategic initiative lost permanently

For the Commonwealth:

  • Egypt saved; Suez Canal secure
  • Time bought for massive reinforcements (American M4 Sherman tanks, new formations, supplies)
  • Confidence partially restored (especially 9th Australian Division's successes)
  • Combined-arms tactics improving (though still flawed, as El Mreir demonstrated)
  • Air superiority maintained and growing

Auchinleck's defensive battle, though ending in stalemate, was a strategic masterpiece. He stabilized the front after catastrophic Gazala defeat, prevented breakthrough to Cairo, and inflicted attrition Axis could not sustain. His relief in August was politically driven rather than militarily justified—Montgomery inherited Auchinleck's success.

Lessons Learned

For the British/Commonwealth:

  1. Defensive Success: British forces fought effectively in prepared defensive positions with combined-arms integration
  2. Australian Excellence: 9th Australian Division's night attacks demonstrated Allied forces could execute offensive operations successfully
  3. Combined Arms Essential: Ruweisat Ridge failures showed tanks and infantry MUST attack together; separation led to disaster
  4. Tank Vulnerability: El Mreir massacre demonstrated tanks advancing without support into prepared AT defenses faced annihilation
  5. Attrition Advantage: British could replace losses; Axis could not—attrition strategy finally working
  6. Leadership Matters: Auchinleck's calm, competent command stabilized situation after Gazala disaster

For the Germans:

  1. Offensive Limits Reached: Extended supply lines, fuel shortages, and exhaustion made further offensive impossible
  2. Numerical Inferiority Decisive: German tactical superiority couldn't overcome growing Allied material advantages
  3. Cannot Hold Forever: Defensive positions at Alamein strong, but British numerical superiority meant eventual defeat inevitable
  4. Supply Crisis Critical: Malta interdiction and extended logistics made sustained operations impossible

For the Italians:

  1. Vulnerability to Night Attacks: Australian night operations particularly effective against Italian defensive positions
  2. Equipment Obsolescence: Italian tanks and AT guns increasingly obsolete against improving Allied equipment
  3. Heavy Casualties: Italian formations suffered disproportionate losses, particularly in Australian attacks

Tactical Innovations:

  • Australian Night Fighting: 9th Australian Division's night assault tactics became model for future operations
  • Combined Brigade Groups: Auchinleck's reorganization into integrated brigade groups (tanks, infantry, artillery, AT guns) copied German tactics successfully
  • Defense in Depth: British finally employed depth in defense rather than linear positions
  • El Mreir Lesson: Demonstrated danger of attacking into prepared AT defenses without proper reconnaissance, artillery preparation, and combined-arms coordination

The Historical Significance

First El Alamein deserves recognition as the true turning point of the North African campaign, though overshadowed by Montgomery's later Second Alamein victory (October 1942). Auchinleck's defensive battle:

  • Saved Egypt when defeat appeared certain
  • Halted Rommel's winning streak permanently
  • Bought time for overwhelming Allied buildup
  • Demonstrated improving British tactical competence
  • Established defensive positions Montgomery would use for Second Alamein offensive

Churchill later acknowledged: "Before Alamein we never had a victory; after Alamein we never had a defeat." But the "Alamein" that mattered strategically was Auchinleck's First Alamein—the defensive battle that stopped Rommel and shifted the strategic balance irreversibly.

The battle established the pattern for the remainder of the desert war: British material superiority grinding down tactically superior but numerically inferior and logistically starved Axis forces. Rommel's genius could no longer overcome mathematics—and First El Alamein was where the mathematics became decisive.
