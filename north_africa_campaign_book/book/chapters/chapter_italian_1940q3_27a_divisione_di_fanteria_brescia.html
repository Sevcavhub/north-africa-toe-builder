North Africa Campaign 1940-1943: Complete Order of Battle

27ª Divisione di Fanteria "Brescia"

Nation: Italian Quarter: 1940-Q3 (July-September 1940) Organization Level: Division Parent Formation: XXI Corpo d'Armata

1. Overview

The 27ª Divisione di Fanteria "Brescia" stood alongside the Pavia Division as one of Italy's binary autotrasportabile infantry divisions deployed to Libya's western sector during summer 1940. Generale di Divisione Giuseppe Cremascoli's 7,450 soldiers—reduced from pre-1939 ternary divisions' 13,000—occupied positions near Zawiya, west of Tripoli, defending against potential French attack from Tunisia while preparing for Mussolini's invasion of Egypt.

By September 1940, Brescia Division embodied the same Italian military paradox as sister division Pavia: adequate training and morale undermined by obsolete equipment and insufficient mechanization. Armed with identical equipment (4,980 Carcano M1891 rifles, 216 Breda M30 LMGs, 12 L3/35 tankettes, 8 obsolete 47mm AT guns), Brescia faced modern British armor with weapons designed for Ethiopian colonial warfare. The division's 312 trucks and 12 machine-gun-armed tankettes represented "mechanization" wholly inadequate for modern combined-arms warfare.

Operation E—Italy's invasion of Egypt (September 13-16, 1940)—saw Brescia Division participate in the timid advance to Sidi Barrani. Unlike Pavia which advanced along the coast, Brescia operated in inland sectors as part of Marshal Graziani's six-division offensive. When British Operation Compass (December 9-11, 1940) struck, Brescia Division was annihilated alongside Pavia and three other divisions—36,000 Italian prisoners in three days, Tenth Army destroyed.

2. Command: Generale di Divisione Giuseppe Cremascoli

Appointment: June 1, 1940 (on eve of Italy's entry into WWII) Rank: Generale di Divisione (Major General) Previous Service: Italian Army Staff Background: Professional officer, administrative experience Fate: Captured during Operation Compass (December 1940)

Giuseppe Cremascoli assumed command of Brescia Division in June 1940—days before Italy declared war on France and Britain (June 10, 1940). His immediate task was preparing the division for combat operations: completing peacetime training, integrating reservists, positioning for potential operations against French Tunisia or British Egypt.

During summer 1940, Cremascoli commanded defensive operations in Zawiya sector—establishing positions, coordinating with neighboring divisions (Pavia to the west, other units to the east), and preparing for Mussolini's ordered invasion of Egypt. When Operation E commenced (September 13, 1940), Cremascoli led the division in inland advance toward Sidi Barrani—encountering minimal British resistance as covering forces withdrew.

Graziani's decision to halt at Sidi Barrani and establish static defensive camps proved disastrous for Cremascoli and his division. For three months (September-December 1940), Brescia occupied exposed positions in the defensive camp system—separate fortified positions unable to provide mutual support, vulnerable to British breakthrough and encirclement.

When Operation Compass struck (December 9-11, 1940), British forces annihilated five Italian divisions including Brescia. Cremascoli was captured along with thousands of his soldiers—Brescia Division ceased to exist as combat formation. His brief command (June-December 1940) reflected Italian military tragedy: competent officer given impossible mission with inadequate equipment and hopeless strategic situation.

3. Organization and Equipment

Binary Division Structure

The Brescia Division's organization mirrored Pavia and other binary autotrasportabile divisions—product of 1938-1939 Italian Army reforms reducing divisions from ternary (three infantry regiments, 13,000 personnel) to binary (two regiments, 7,500 personnel). Goal was creating more divisions with limited manpower—quantity over quality.

Two Infantry Regiments:

  • 19° Reggimento Fanteria "Brescia" (2,180 personnel)
  • 20° Reggimento Fanteria "Brescia" (2,180 personnel)

Each regiment fielded two battalions (vs three in ternary divisions), each battalion comprising three rifle companies plus heavy weapons company (machine guns, mortars). Binary structure reduced combat power 33% compared to ternary divisions.

Supporting Arms:

  • 55° Reggimento Artiglieria "Brescia" (1,650 personnel, 36 guns)
  • XXVII Battaglione Mitraglieri (Machine Gun Battalion, 420 personnel)
  • XXVII Battaglione Genio Misto (Mixed Engineer Battalion, 380 personnel)
  • XXVII Compagnia Controcarro (Anti-Tank Company, 142 personnel, 8 × 47mm guns)
  • XXVII Battaglione Carri (27th Tank Battalion, 196 personnel, 12 × L3/35 tankettes)
  • Medical Section (124 personnel)
  • Supply Section (178 personnel)

Autotrasportabile designation meant division could be moved by truck if corps allocated transport—operational mobility between foot-mobile and fully motorized divisions.

Infantry Weapons (Identical to Pavia)

Carcano M1891 Rifle (4,980 rifles): WWI-vintage bolt-action rifle, 6.5×52mm Carcano caliber, 6-round fixed magazine. Adequate but inferior to British Lee-Enfield (10-round magazine, faster bolt action, more powerful .303 cartridge). Effective range 300-400 meters, reliable but underpowered.

Breda M30 Light Machine Gun (216 guns): Notoriously problematic weapon. 20-round magazine (too small), required oiling cartridges (attracted dust/sand), tool-required barrel change, prone to jamming. Each 10-man squad built around single Breda M30, but weapon unreliability undermined Italian firepower. British Bren gun vastly superior.

Breda M37 Heavy Machine Gun (48 guns): Reliable heavy machine gun, 8×59mm Breda caliber, strip-fed, 450 rounds/minute. Equipped machine gun battalion and regimental heavy weapons companies. Effective sustained fire weapon for defensive positions.

Artillery (48 Guns - Identical to Pavia)

100/17 Mod. 14 Howitzer (12 guns): WWI-vintage 100mm howitzer, maximum range 10,300 meters, firing 13.2kg HE shells. Three 4-gun batteries (I Gruppo, 55° Reggimento Artiglieria). Adequate indirect fire support but limited by ammunition shortages.

75/27 Mod. 06 Field Gun (24 guns): Obsolete 75mm gun dating from 1906, maximum range 10,400 meters, firing 6.5kg shells. Two 12-gun groups (II & III Gruppi, 55° Reggimento). Outranged and outgunned by British 25-pounder (13,400m range, 11.3kg shell). Ammunition conservation constrained fire missions.

47/32 Mod. 35 Anti-Tank Gun (8 guns): Standard Italian AT weapon—woefully inadequate against British Matildas. Maximum penetration 43mm at 500 meters. British Matilda II: 78mm frontal armor—Italian 47mm shells ineffective. Division's sole AT defense against British armor: hopeless.

20/65 Mod. 35 Anti-Aircraft Gun (4 guns): 20mm Breda AA gun, effective ceiling 2,000 meters, 220-240 rpm. Minimal air defense—RAF enjoyed air superiority, Italian AA inadequate.

Tanks and Vehicles

L3/35 Tankette (12 vehicles, 10 operational): Two-man tankette armed with twin 8mm machine guns, 6-15mm armor, 42 km/h maximum speed. Against infantry: mobile firepower. Against British tanks: death trap—15mm armor penetrated by 2-pounder guns at 1,000+ meters, machine guns useless against armor. During Operation Compass: abandoned or destroyed.

Autoblinda AB 40 (8 armored cars, 7 operational): Four-wheeled reconnaissance vehicle, twin 8mm machine guns, 9mm armor, 78 km/h road speed. Adequate for scouting but vulnerable to AT weapons.

Trucks (312 total): Fiat 626N (142 trucks, 3-ton), FIAT-SPA 38R (85 light trucks), FIAT-SPA CL-39 (45 light trucks), SPA AS.37 Sahariano (40 desert vehicles). Insufficient for simultaneous division movement—autotrasportabile required corps transport allocation.

4. Operations: Border Security and Operation E

Tunisian Border Operations (June-August 1940)

Following Italy's declaration of war (June 10, 1940), Brescia Division secured Tunisian-Libyan border against potential French attack from Tunisia. French Tunisia remained under Vichy control after France's surrender (June 22, 1940), creating uneasy frontier situation. Brescia conducted border patrols, established defensive positions, and monitored French forces.

By August 1940, French threat diminished—Vichy cooperation with Axis removed immediate danger. Brescia shifted focus to preparing for invasion of Egypt, repositioning from western Libya toward coastal advance routes.

Operation E: Italian Invasion of Egypt (September 13-16, 1940)

Mussolini ordered invasion September 13, 1940. Brescia Division advanced with six-division Tenth Army toward Sidi Barrani—100km into Egypt. Unlike Pavia (coastal advance), Brescia operated in inland sectors, advancing through desert terrain south of coastal Via Balbia road.

British covering forces withdrew, demolishing wells and supply dumps. Italian advance was slow: foot infantry marched 15-20km daily, motor transport shuttled supplies, water shortages limited operations. By September 16, Italian forces occupied Sidi Barrani—Graziani halted offensive, established defensive camps.

Brescia Division occupied positions in defensive camp system—separate fortified camps several kilometers apart, vulnerable to defeat in detail. For three months (September-December 1940), the division endured static defense: chronic supply shortages, declining morale, realization that offensive had stalled.

Operation Compass Destruction (December 9-11, 1940)

British Operation Compass shattered Italian defenses with overwhelming force. British Matilda tanks—invulnerable to Italian 47mm AT guns—overran defensive camps while artillery bombarded positions. Brescia Division fought desperately but was overwhelmed: thousands killed/wounded, division commander Cremascoli captured, survivors taken prisoner.

In three days, British forces destroyed five Italian divisions including Brescia—36,000 prisoners, complete Tenth Army collapse. Brescia Division ceased to exist as combat formation. Equipment losses were total: 12 L3/35 tankettes destroyed/abandoned, 48 artillery pieces captured, 312 trucks destroyed/captured.

7. Tactical Doctrine and Known Limitations

Autotrasportabile Concept

Brescia Division balanced foot mobility with truck transport—division marched on foot for local operations, or was moved by truck if corps allocated transport. However, 312 divisional trucks insufficient for simultaneous movement of 7,450 personnel. Without corps transport, division moved in echelons or foot-marched.

Defensive Doctrine

Italian defensive doctrine emphasized prepared positions, minefields, wire obstacles, mutually-supporting strongpoints. However, Sidi Barrani camps violated doctrine—camps separated by kilometers, unable to provide mutual support, defeated separately by British.

Known Limitations (Same as Pavia)

Officers recognized critical deficiencies:

  • Inadequate AT weapons vs British armor (47mm ineffective against Matildas)
  • Obsolete tanks (L3/35 useless in tank combat)
  • Insufficient motor transport (dependent on corps allocation)
  • Weak AA defense (RAF dominance)
  • Binary structure (33% reduced combat power)

These limitations proved fatal during Operation Compass.

8. Legacy and Historical Significance

Operation Compass Annihilation

Brescia Division's destruction during Operation Compass epitomized Italian military failure in North Africa: adequate soldiers hamstrung by obsolete equipment, insufficient logistics, incompetent strategy. British forces with superior tanks, better logistics, and aggressive leadership destroyed five divisions in three days.

Brescia casualties: thousands killed/wounded, most of 7,450 personnel captured including commander Cremascoli, all equipment lost. Division rebuilt 1941 but participated in subsequent disasters—ultimately destroyed again during Axis final collapse in Tunisia (May 1943).

Lessons

Italian experience demonstrated that morale and training cannot overcome equipment inferiority. Brescia's soldiers fought as well as circumstances allowed, but 47mm guns could not penetrate Matilda armor, L3/35 tankettes could not engage British tanks, obsolete artillery could not suppress British positions.

German Afrika Korps arrival (February 1941) prevented complete Italian collapse, but Axis defeat was inevitable given logistics constraints and Allied material superiority.
