North Africa Campaign 1940-1943: Complete Order of Battle

2nd South African Division

Nation: British (South African) Quarter: 1942-Q1 Organization Level: Division

1. Overview

The 2nd South African Infantry Division was a Commonwealth formation that served with distinction in North Africa before its tragic destruction at Tobruk on June 21, 1942. During the first quarter of 1942 (January-March), the division occupied defensive positions along the Gazala Line in eastern Libya, preparing for what would become the climactic battles of mid-1942.

Raised in South Africa in 1940, the 2nd SA Division represented the Union's second major contribution to the Allied war effort in North Africa. Unlike its sister formation, the 1st SA Division, which had fought in the East African campaign, the 2nd SA Division was deployed directly to the Western Desert, arriving in Libya in late 1941.

By early 1942, the division had been integrated into XIII Corps of the British Eighth Army, occupying defensive boxes in the Gazala Line alongside British and Commonwealth divisions. The division's composition reflected South Africa's racially segregated military system, with both European (white) and non-European (African) personnel serving in separate units, though the exact proportions are not documented for Q1 1942.

Key Statistics (Q1 1942):

  • Total Strength: 14,000 personnel
  • Organization: 2 infantry brigades + divisional troops
  • Artillery: 96 guns (72× 25-pdr, 24× 40mm Bofors)
  • Armored Cars: 40 (Marmon-Herrington Mk II/III)
  • Motor Vehicles: 2,200 trucks and support vehicles
  • Parent Formation: XIII Corps, British Eighth Army
  • Headquarters Location: Gazala Line, Libya

2. Command Structure

Division Commander

The identity of the division commander during Q1 1942 is not recorded in available source documents. The position would have been held by an officer of Major-General rank, typical for Commonwealth infantry divisions of this period.

Division Staff

The division headquarters staff comprised approximately 500 personnel:

  • Officers: 70 (including operations, intelligence, logistics, and signals staff)
  • NCOs: 150 (section chiefs and administrative staff)
  • Enlisted: 280 (clerks, drivers, guards, and support personnel)

The Chief of Staff (GSO 1) position was typically held by a Lieutenant-Colonel who coordinated all divisional operations and planning.

Headquarters Location

Division headquarters was established along the Gazala Line in defensive positions, maintaining close liaison with XIII Corps headquarters to the east. The static defensive nature of the Gazala position allowed for more permanent headquarters facilities than typical desert mobile operations.

Historical Context

The division would later come under command of XIII Corps during the Gazala battles (May-June 1942), where it was forced to withdraw into Tobruk fortress. The division was captured in its entirety when Tobruk fell on June 21, 1942, marking one of the most devastating British defeats of the North African campaign. Approximately 33,000 Allied troops, including most of the 2nd SA Division, surrendered that day.

This TO&E snapshot from Q1 1942 represents the division in its final complete state before the catastrophe at Tobruk.

3. Personnel Strength

Total Strength: 14,000 Personnel

The 2nd South African Division fielded approximately 14,000 personnel, representing its establishment strength for a Commonwealth infantry division. However, by early 1942, the division was already showing signs of being understrength, having received composite units from the 1st SA Division (notably the Blake Group) to fill gaps.

Officer Corps: 900 Officers (6.4% of total)

The division's officer corps included:

  • Division staff and headquarters officers
  • Brigade and battalion commanders
  • Company and platoon leaders
  • Technical specialists (artillery, engineers, signals, medical officers)
  • South African officers maintained the British military tradition, with most company-level officers coming from European South African backgrounds

Non-Commissioned Officers: 2,800 NCOs (20% of total)

NCOs formed the critical leadership backbone:

  • Platoon sergeants and section leaders
  • Artillery gun crew chiefs
  • Technical specialists in signals and engineering
  • Administrative and supply NCOs
  • Many experienced NCOs had transferred from the 1st SA Division after the East African campaign

Enlisted Personnel: 10,300 (73.6% of total)

The bulk of the division consisted of enlisted soldiers:

  • Infantry riflemen (Lee-Enfield armed)
  • Machine gun crews (Bren and Vickers)
  • Artillery crews
  • Drivers and mechanics
  • Engineers and pioneers
  • Medical and supply personnel

Personnel Quality and Composition

The 2nd SA Division was less experienced than the 1st SA Division, having missed the victorious East African campaign. Many soldiers had basic training in South Africa but limited combat experience as of Q1 1942. The Gazala Line defensive period (January-May 1942) would be their primary exposure to combat conditions before the disaster at Tobruk.

Racial Composition: Like all South African military formations, the division included both European (white) and non-European (African) personnel. Based on proportions from the 1st SA Division (approximately 5,000 of 17,000 non-European), an estimated 4,000 non-Europeans served in the 2nd SA Division, primarily in transport, labor, and service roles, though some served in combat support functions.

Morale: Morale was generally good in Q1 1942, with the division's defensive role in prepared positions providing a degree of security. However, awareness of being understrength and receiving composite units from the 1st SA Division may have caused some concern about the division's readiness for major combat.

5. Infantry Weapons

The 2nd SA Division was equipped with standard British Commonwealth infantry weapons, supplied through British ordnance depots in Egypt.

Rifles: 8,500 Total

Lee-Enfield No. 1 Mk III Rifle

  • Count: 8,500 rifles
  • Specifications:
    • Caliber: .303 British (7.7×56mm)
    • Action: Bolt-action
    • Magazine: 10-round detachable box
    • Effective Range: 500 meters (sights to 1,800 meters)
    • Rate of Fire: 15-30 aimed rounds per minute
    • Weight: 4.19 kg

The Lee-Enfield was the standard rifle for all Commonwealth forces. South African troops were thoroughly trained in its use, with many having experience from pre-war militia service. The robust design handled desert conditions well with proper maintenance.

Light Machine Guns: 450 Total

Bren Light Machine Gun

  • Count: 450 guns
  • Specifications:
    • Caliber: .303 British
    • Action: Gas-operated, magazine-fed
    • Magazine: 30-round detachable curved box
    • Effective Range: 600 meters
    • Cyclic Rate: 500-520 rounds/minute
    • Weight: 10.35 kg (gun only)

The Bren Gun provided squad-level automatic fire support. Each infantry platoon typically had 2-3 Bren guns, making them the primary close-range suppressive fire weapon. The Bren's reliability and accuracy made it one of the war's best light machine guns.

Medium Machine Guns: 48 Total

Vickers Medium Machine Gun

  • Count: 48 guns
  • Specifications:
    • Caliber: .303 British
    • Action: Recoil-operated, water-cooled
    • Feed: 250-round fabric belt
    • Effective Range: 2,000 meters
    • Cyclic Rate: 450-500 rounds/minute
    • Weight: 18.14 kg (gun only), 50 kg (with tripod, water, and ammunition)

Vickers guns were organized in the Die Middellandse Regiment machine gun battalion, providing heavy sustained fire support for defensive positions. These weapons were ideal for the Gazala Line's static defensive posture, where they could be set up with overhead cover and pre-registered fire zones.

Additional Infantry Weapons

While not quantified in source documents, the division would also have been equipped with:

  • 2-inch Mortars: Light mortars at platoon level for close fire support
  • 3-inch Mortars: Company-level mortars for heavier indirect fire
  • Boys Anti-Tank Rifles: Limited numbers for infantry anti-tank defense (mostly obsolete by 1942)
  • Grenades: Mills bombs (fragmentation), smoke grenades, anti-tank grenades
  • Bayonets: Pattern 1907 bayonets for all rifles

6. Armored Reconnaissance

Marmon-Herrington Armored Cars: 40 Total

The 7th South African Reconnaissance Battalion operated locally-produced South African armored cars, designed and manufactured by Marmon-Herrington in Johannesburg specifically for desert warfare.

Marmon-Herrington Mk II Armored Car

  • Count: 28 (25 operational)
  • Specifications:
    • Weight: 6 tons
    • Armament:
      • 1× Boys .55 anti-tank rifle or 1× Vickers .303 MG (turret)
      • 1× Bren .303 LMG (hull)
    • Armor: 12mm maximum
    • Engine: Ford V8 95 HP gasoline
    • Speed: 80 km/h road, 40 km/h cross-country
    • Range: 320 km
    • Crew: 4 (commander, driver, gunner, radio operator)
    • Drive: 4×4 all-wheel drive

The Mk II was the initial production model, featuring a two-axle design with good cross-country performance. Its light armament limited its combat effectiveness, primarily suitable for reconnaissance and screening roles.

Marmon-Herrington Mk III Armored Car

  • Count: 12 (11 operational)
  • Specifications:
    • Weight: 6.5 tons
    • Armament:
      • 1× 2-pdr (40mm) gun (turret) OR 1× Vickers .303 MG
      • 1-2× Bren .303 LMG (hull/AA)
    • Armor: 12mm maximum
    • Engine: Ford V8 95 HP gasoline
    • Speed: 75 km/h road, 35 km/h cross-country
    • Range: 300 km
    • Crew: 4
    • Drive: 4×4 all-wheel drive

The Mk III was an improved model with some vehicles mounting a 2-pounder gun, providing anti-tank capability. However, most Mk IIIs in the 2nd SA Division retained machine gun armament.

Employment and Effectiveness

The 7th SA Reconnaissance Battalion's armored cars performed:

  • Forward screening: 10-15 km ahead of main defensive positions
  • Flank security: Patrolling the desert flanks of the Gazala Line
  • Liaison: Communications between divisions and corps headquarters
  • Observation: Reporting enemy movements and strength

Known Issues:

  • Light armament ineffective against enemy armored vehicles
  • Thin armor vulnerable to anti-tank guns and tank fire
  • Limited operational radius required frequent refueling
  • Mechanical reliability issues with V8 engines in desert conditions
  • Radio equipment sometimes unreliable, limiting reconnaissance reporting

The South African-built armored cars were adequate for reconnaissance but completely outmatched by German armored cars (SdKfz 222, 232) and light tanks. Their primary value was mobility and the crew's eyes, not combat power.

7. Artillery

Total Artillery: 96 Guns

The 2nd SA Division's artillery strength centered on three field regiments equipped with the British 25-pounder gun, plus a light anti-aircraft regiment with Bofors guns.

Field Artillery: 72 Guns

Ordnance QF 25-Pounder Gun-Howitzer

  • Count: 72 guns (70 operational)
  • Organization: 3 regiments of 24 guns each (3 batteries per regiment, 8 guns per battery)
  • Units:
    • 2nd South African Field Regiment (24 guns)
    • 3rd South African Field Regiment (24 guns)
    • 6th South African Field Regiment (24 guns)
  • Specifications:
    • Caliber: 87.6mm (3.45 inches)
    • Type: Gun-howitzer (dual-purpose)
    • Range: 12,250 meters (normal charge), 13,400 meters (supercharge)
    • Shell Weight: 11.34 kg
    • Rate of Fire: 8 rounds/minute (sustained), 20 rounds/minute (maximum)
    • Muzzle Velocity: 518 m/s
    • Traverse: 4° left, 4° right (on carriage)
    • Elevation: -5° to +40°
    • Weight: 1,800 kg (firing), 1,630 kg (travel)
    • Tow Vehicle: Quad tractor or 3-ton truck
    • Crew: 6 (detachment commander, layers, loaders, ammunition handlers)

Ammunition Types:

  • HE (High Explosive): Standard anti-personnel and light fortification round
  • AP (Armor-Piercing): Limited anti-tank capability, rarely used by 1942
  • Smoke: White phosphorus for screening
  • Illuminating: Star shells for night operations

The 25-pounder was the British Army's primary field artillery piece, combining the roles of field gun (flat trajectory) and howitzer (high-angle fire). Its versatility, reliability, and excellent fire control system made it one of the war's best artillery pieces.

Employment: The three field regiments provided divisional artillery support, capable of firing concentrated bombardments under divisional control or supporting individual brigades. In the Gazala Line's static defensive posture, guns could be pre-registered on likely enemy approaches and coordinates, maximizing effectiveness.

Anti-Aircraft Artillery: 24 Guns

Bofors 40mm L60 Light Anti-Aircraft Gun

  • Count: 24 guns (23 operational)
  • Organization: 2nd SA Light Anti-Aircraft Regiment (3 batteries, 8 guns per battery)
  • Specifications:
    • Caliber: 40mm (1.57 inches)
    • Type: Automatic cannon
    • Effective Ceiling: 3,650 meters (vertical), 1,200 meters (horizontal effective range)
    • Shell Weight: 0.96 kg
    • Rate of Fire: 120 rounds/minute (cyclic)
    • Muzzle Velocity: 874 m/s
    • Ammunition Feed: 4-round clips
    • Weight: 1,980 kg (firing position)
    • Tow Vehicle: 3-ton truck
    • Crew: 5-6

The Bofors 40mm was the Allies' standard light anti-aircraft gun, Swedish-designed but produced under license in Britain. Its automatic operation and high rate of fire made it effective against low-flying aircraft.

Employment: The 24 guns provided air defense for:

  • Division headquarters and command posts
  • Artillery positions
  • Supply dumps and logistics areas
  • Road junctions and key terrain
  • Bridges and defiles

By Q1 1942, the Luftwaffe's presence in North Africa was increasing, making air defense a critical concern. The Bofors' dual capability against aircraft and ground targets (including light armored vehicles) added value.

Anti-Tank Artillery

Source documents note that anti-tank guns were deployed at battalion level but not included in divisional artillery totals. These would typically have been:

  • 2-pounder (40mm) anti-tank guns: Company and battalion-level organic AT weapons
  • 6-pounder (57mm) anti-tank guns: If available (began replacing 2-pdrs in early 1942)

The exact numbers and types are not documented for Q1 1942, but a standard Commonwealth division would have had 48-72 anti-tank guns distributed among infantry battalions, anti-tank platoons, and brigade-level anti-tank companies.

9. Supply and Logistics

Supply Status (Q1 1942)

As of early 1942, the 2nd SA Division's supply situation was adequate for defensive operations but vulnerable to disruption. The division occupied prepared positions along the Gazala Line with established supply dumps, but the overall strategic situation was precarious.

Fuel Supply

Daily Requirement: 12,000-15,000 gallons for normal operations

  • Sources: British Eighth Army ordnance depots, supplied via Tobruk and coastal ports
  • Delivery: Road convoys from depots to divisional fuel points, then to brigade and battalion dumps
  • Storage: Brigade dumps held 3-5 days of fuel supplies
  • Constraints: Axis air and submarine attacks on Mediterranean convoys limited overall theater fuel availability

Ammunition Supply

Types Required:

  • British .303 rifle and machine gun ammunition
  • 25-pounder artillery shells (HE, smoke, illuminating)
  • 40mm Bofors ammunition
  • 2-inch and 3-inch mortar bombs
  • Anti-tank gun ammunition
  • Grenades and small arms ammunition

Resupply:

  • British Eighth Army ordnance depots in Egypt
  • Brigade dumps held 3-5 days of combat supplies
  • Critical shortages developed during Gazala battles (May-June 1942) due to Axis interdiction

Water Supply

Critical Constraint: Desert operations made water the most vital supply commodity.

Daily Requirements:

  • Minimum: 1 gallon per man (14,000 gallons for drinking only)
  • Actual: 2-3 gallons per man for drinking, cooking, washing, vehicle cooling
  • Division daily need: 30,000-45,000 gallons

Transport:

  • Water transported in jerrycans and water trucks
  • All water brought from rear areas (no local sources in desert)
  • Quality control critical to prevent dysentery and waterborne diseases

Rationing:

  • Strict water discipline enforced
  • Priority: drinking water for personnel
  • Secondary: vehicle radiators (critical in desert heat)
  • Minimal allocation for washing and sanitation

Operational Radius

Limitation: Approximately 150-200 miles from supply dumps with organic transport

  • Factors:
    • Truck capacity and fuel consumption
    • Water transport capacity
    • Road network availability
    • Enemy interdiction

Q1 1942 Reality: Division in static defensive positions, so operational radius less relevant than in mobile operations. However, the limitation would become critical during the May-June 1942 Gazala battles when the division was forced to maneuver.

Supply Deterioration (May-June 1942)

The supply situation would drastically deteriorate during the Gazala battles:

  • Axis interdiction of supply lines
  • Division cut off in Tobruk fortress
  • Inadequate stockpiles for sustained siege
  • Failure to maintain supply lines led directly to Tobruk's fall on June 21, 1942

This Q1 1942 snapshot represents the division in a relatively stable supply situation that would soon collapse under combat pressure.

10. Weather and Environment

Climate (Q1 1942: January-March)

Winter/Early Spring Season:

  • Daytime Temperature: 60-75°F (15-24°C)
  • Nighttime Temperature: 40-50°F (4-10°C)
  • Daily Temperature Swing: 30-50°F

This was the most favorable time of year for operations in the Western Desert. Cooler temperatures reduced water requirements and made physical exertion more tolerable.

Terrain

North African Desert and Coastal Plain:

  • Operations in Gazala Line and Tobruk areas
  • Rocky desert with limited vegetation
  • Surface: mix of hard-packed sand, gravel, rocks
  • Occasional wadis (dry riverbeds) providing natural defensive positions
  • Coastal plain transitioning to escarpment terrain

Defensive Implications:

  • "Box" defensive positions could be prepared with minefields and wire
  • Limited natural cover made camouflage and concealment difficult
  • Observation excellent in clear desert air (visibility 10+ miles)
  • Dust clouds visible for many miles, revealing troop movements

Weather Challenges

Winter (Q1):

  • Occasional rain (rare but significant when it occurred)
  • Cooler temperatures improved operating conditions for troops
  • Reduced water requirements
  • Better conditions for vehicle engines (less overheating)

Spring (approaching Q2):

  • Increasing heat as season progressed
  • Sandstorms (khamsin winds) becoming more frequent
  • Water scarcity becoming more critical
  • Equipment overheating issues increasing

Environmental Challenges

Constant Issues:

  • Navigation: Featureless terrain required compass navigation and careful map-reading
  • Dust and Sand: Affected engines, weapons, requiring frequent maintenance
    • Air filters clogged rapidly
    • Weapons required daily cleaning
    • Lubricants attracted sand, causing wear
  • Extreme Temperature Variations: Daily swings required troops to cope with both heat and cold
  • Limited Water Sources: All water had to be transported
  • Disease Vectors: Flies abundant, spreading dysentery and other diseases
  • Desert Sores: Skin conditions from sand, dust, and limited washing water
  • Wildlife: Scorpions and snakes posed danger in defensive positions

June 1942 Conditions (Looking Ahead)

By June 1942, when the division would be engaged in the Gazala battles and Tobruk siege, conditions had worsened dramatically:

  • Daytime temperatures: 90-95°F (32-35°C)
  • Sandstorms reduced visibility to meters
  • Water shortages exacerbated by combat tempo
  • Heat exhaustion became a significant casualty factor

11. Combat History

Formation and Deployment

The 2nd South African Division was raised in South Africa in 1940 as part of the Union's contribution to the Allied war effort. Unlike the 1st SA Division, which fought in the successful East African campaign against Italian forces, the 2nd SA Division was deployed directly to the Western Desert theater.

The division arrived in Libya in late 1941 and was integrated into XIII Corps of the British Eighth Army. By early 1942, it occupied defensive positions along the Gazala Line, a system of fortified "boxes" extending from the coast southward into the desert.

Gazala Line Defenses (Q1-Q2 1942)

During the first quarter of 1942, the division's primary mission was defense of its assigned sector of the Gazala Line. This static defensive role involved:

  • Construction and improvement of fortified positions
  • Laying of minefields
  • Patrolling between defensive boxes
  • Training in defensive tactics
  • Limited offensive patrolling

The relative quiet of Q1 1942 would be shattered in late May when Rommel launched Operation Venezia (the Gazala offensive).

Battle of Gazala (May 26 - June 14, 1942)

Role: Defense of Gazala Line positions

Though this battle occurred after the Q1 1942 period covered by this document, it represents the division's first and last major battle:

  • May 26-June 14: Division engaged in defensive battles along the Gazala Line
  • Outcome: Forced withdrawal after Axis breakthrough
  • Casualties: Moderate during fighting withdrawal
  • Division fell back to Tobruk perimeter as British Eighth Army retreated into Egypt

Tobruk Garrison (June 15-21, 1942)

The Final Battle:

After the collapse of the Gazala Line, the 2nd SA Division withdrew into Tobruk fortress alongside other Commonwealth units. British higher command made the fateful decision to hold Tobruk rather than evacuate it, despite the fortress being poorly stocked for a siege and surrounded by advancing German-Italian forces.

June 15-20, 1942: Division occupied defensive positions in Tobruk perimeter June 21, 1942: German-Italian assault captured Tobruk

Result: Approximately 33,000 Allied troops captured, including most of the 2nd South African Division. The division effectively ceased to exist as a combat formation. This represented one of the worst British defeats of the North African campaign and a devastating blow to South African military prestige.

Combat Effectiveness Assessment

Strengths:

  • Good morale and unit cohesion (prior to Tobruk)
  • Adequate training in defensive tactics
  • Standard Commonwealth equipment and organization
  • Competent junior leadership

Weaknesses:

  • Limited combat experience compared to veteran divisions
  • Understrength (missing third brigade, receiving composite units)
  • Vulnerable supply situation
  • Static defensive mindset unsuited to fluid desert warfare
  • Inadequate anti-tank capability for 1942 battlefield

The Tobruk Disaster:

The fall of Tobruk was primarily a failure of British higher command rather than the 2nd SA Division's fighting quality:

  • Decision to defend Tobruk was strategically flawed
  • Fortress inadequately stocked for siege
  • Division and other garrison units were not prepared for prolonged defense
  • Rapid German assault overwhelmed defenses before adequate preparations possible

The division fought competently during the Gazala battles but was placed in an untenable position at Tobruk through no fault of its own.

12. Tactical Doctrine and Capabilities

Infantry Tactics

The 2nd SA Division employed standard British Commonwealth infantry tactics:

Defensive Doctrine:

  • Fortified "box" positions with all-around defense
  • Minefields and wire obstacles covering approaches
  • Mutually supporting positions with overlapping fields of fire
  • Artillery and mortar fire pre-registered on likely enemy axes
  • Reserve companies held for counter-attacks

Offensive Doctrine (Limited Use):

  • Set-piece attacks with artillery preparation
  • Infantry-tank cooperation (when armor available)
  • Emphasis on fire and movement at section and platoon level
  • Night attacks when possible to reduce casualties

Combined Arms Integration

Artillery Support:

  • Three field regiments provided responsive fire support
  • Capability for divisional-level concentrated fire missions
  • Forward observers with infantry battalions
  • Pre-registered defensive fire zones

Anti-Aircraft Defense:

  • Light AA regiment provided air defense umbrella
  • Bofors guns could also engage light armored vehicles

Reconnaissance:

  • 7th SA Reconnaissance Battalion screened approaches
  • Armored cars provided early warning
  • Limited capability against enemy armor

Engineering Support:

  • Two field companies provided:
    • Minefield laying and breaching
    • Obstacle construction

14. Historical Significance

Strategic Importance

The 2nd South African Division's brief combat career and tragic end at Tobruk had significant strategic and political implications:

Military Impact:

  • Loss of 14,000 trained Commonwealth troops
  • Psychological blow to Allied morale in North Africa
  • Demonstrated British vulnerability in desert warfare
  • Reinforced German operational superiority in mobile operations

Political Impact:

  • Severe blow to South African morale and military reputation
  • Increased criticism of British higher command in South Africa
  • Strained South African government's support for the war effort
  • Led to extensive reforms in Commonwealth force employment

Lessons Learned

The destruction of the 2nd SA Division at Tobruk provided hard lessons:

Operational:

  • Do not defend isolated fortresses in mobile warfare environment
  • Ensure adequate stockpiles before accepting siege conditions
  • Maintain flexibility to withdraw forces when strategically sensible
  • Static defensive thinking unsuited to desert warfare

Tactical:

  • Need for improved anti-tank capabilities
  • Importance of combined-arms training
  • Value of mobile reserves
  • Necessity of adequate air cover

Logistical:

  • Critical importance of secure supply lines
  • Vulnerability of extended defensive lines to encirclement
  • Need for pre-positioned supply dumps in fortress positions

Memorial and Remembrance

The 2nd South African Division is remembered in South Africa as a tragic example of sacrifice in a poorly-planned operation. The Tobruk surrender remains controversial in South African military history, with debates continuing about whether British command adequately supported the division or needlessly sacrificed it.

Memorials to the division's fallen exist in South Africa and at Tobruk, commemorating the soldiers who fought and died or were captured in the North African desert.
