# 10th Armoured Division

**Nation:** British
**Quarter:** 1942-Q4 (October-December 1942)
**Organization Level:** Division
**Parent Formation:** X Corps, British Eighth Army

## 1. Overview

The 10th Armoured Division entered Second Battle of El Alamein as one of Montgomery's two primary armoured exploitation forces alongside 1st Armoured Division. Formed in August 1941, the division reached North Africa with limited combat experience compared to desert veterans but compensated with fresh equipment and personnel. By October 1942, the division fielded 218 tanks (mix of Sherman, Grant, and Crusader), 84 guns, and 14,450 personnel organized in two armoured brigades (8th and 24th) plus attached lorried infantry.

The division's symbol—a fox's mask—reflected traditional British hunting imagery, appropriate for a formation tasked with pursuing Rommel's defeated forces across Libya. During 1942-Q4, the 10th Armoured fought two critical engagements: Alam Halfa (August-September, defensive victory where attached 22nd Armoured Brigade helped halt Rommel's offensive) and Second El Alamein (October-November, breakthrough offensive and pursuit). The division's mixed American-British tank fleet (72 Shermans, 65 Grants, 25 Crusaders, 56 light tanks) typified late-1942 British armour: transitioning from unreliable British cruiser tanks to superior American Lend-Lease equipment.

Montgomery assigned the 10th Armoured Division to X Corps (alongside 1st Armoured) with mission: exploit XXX Corps infantry breakthrough, engage Axis armoured reserves, pursue defeated enemy westward. The division's performance proved solid if unspectacular—methodical combined-arms operations trading tactical elegance for overwhelming material superiority. British logistics advantage (supply lines from Alexandria 250km vs Axis supply from Tripoli 1,800km) ensured adequate fuel, ammunition, and water for sustained offensive operations.

## 2. Command and Leadership

### Division Commander: Major-General Alexander Hugh Gatehouse

**Appointment:** June 1, 1942
**Nickname:** "Galloping Alec" (for aggressive armoured tactics)
**Background:** Career cavalry officer with armoured doctrine experience

Alexander Hugh Gatehouse commanded the 10th Armoured Division through its formative battles in North Africa. Appointed June 1942 as the division completed organization in Egypt, Gatehouse led the formation through Alam Halfa (defensive victory, September 1942) and Second El Alamein (breakthrough offensive, October-November 1942). His tenure was marked by occasional friction with Montgomery over tactical employment of armour—Gatehouse favored concentrated armoured mass, while Montgomery sought to tie tanks closely to infantry advances.

During El Alamein's Operation Supercharge (November 2-4), Gatehouse famously hesitated before committing his division to breakthrough assault, arguing that inadequate infantry support would result in excessive tank losses to German anti-tank guns. Montgomery overruled him, insisting the breakthrough must proceed. The subsequent attack validated both perspectives: British tanks did suffer heavy losses to 88mm guns and hull-down Panzer IVs, but the concentrated assault achieved breakthrough that forced Axis withdrawal—a strategic success despite tactical casualties.

Gatehouse's command style emphasized preservation of armoured strength for decisive battles rather than attrition. This caution reflected hard-learned lessons from earlier desert defeats where British armour charged German anti-tank screens without adequate support. However, by late 1942 British material superiority meant losses could be replaced—Montgomery's willingness to accept tank casualties as cost of strategic victory ultimately proved correct given logistical realities.

### Brigade Commanders

**8th Armoured Brigade - Brigadier Edward C.N. Custance:** Commanded the division's original armoured brigade, fielding mixed tank fleet at El Alamein: 24 Crusaders, 57 Grants, 31 Shermans. Three armoured regiments plus motor battalion provided combined-arms capability. Custance's brigade fought extensively during breakthrough phase, engaging 15th and 21st Panzer Divisions in prolonged tank duels during the "dogfight" phase (October 25-November 1).

**24th Armoured Brigade:** Equipped with Sherman and Grant tanks, the brigade's three yeomanry regiments (Nottinghamshire Yeomanry "Sherwood Rangers," 41st Royal Tank Regiment, 47th Royal Tank Regiment) represented transition from traditional cavalry to mechanized warfare. The Sherwood Rangers in particular earned distinction for aggressive reconnaissance and exploitation operations.

**133rd Lorried Infantry Brigade:** Attached September 29-November 25, 1942 specifically for El Alamein offensive. Three battalions of Royal Sussex Regiment (2nd, 4th, 5th "Cinque Ports") provided mobile infantry support for armoured operations, mounted in trucks for rapid deployment. Lorried infantry secured flanks, cleared anti-tank positions, and consolidated captured ground while tanks pressed forward.

### Headquarters Organization

Division headquarters (495 personnel: 82 officers, 145 NCOs, 268 enlisted) operated from prepared defensive positions near El Alamein during battle, transitioning to mobile command during pursuit. Staff sections managed complex coordination: two armoured brigades, one lorried infantry brigade, five artillery regiments, engineers, signals, logistics—challenging during fluid breakthrough operations. Communications relied on wireless (No. 19 sets for long-range, No. 11 for brigade nets) supplemented by motorcycle dispatch riders and line communications from static headquarters.

## 3. Battle of Alam Halfa (August 30 - September 5, 1942)

### Rommel's Last Offensive

Before Second El Alamein, the 10th Armoured Division's 22nd Armoured Brigade (attached for battle) participated in defensive victory at Alam Halfa. Rommel attempted flanking maneuver south of El Alamein position, seeking to penetrate British rear and disrupt defenses. Montgomery anticipated the attack, preparing defensive positions on Alam Halfa Ridge with hull-down tanks, anti-tank guns, and concentrated artillery.

The 22nd Armoured Brigade (part of 7th Armoured Division, temporarily under 10th Armoured control) occupied defensive positions south of Alam Halfa Ridge with Grant tanks—the Grant's 75mm sponson gun effective in defensive hull-down positions despite awkward offensive limitations. When Rommel's panzers attacked August 30-31, they encountered prepared defenses, extensive minefields, and concentrated artillery fire. British tanks engaged from defensive positions rather than charging—reversing earlier failed doctrine.

German attacks achieved limited penetration but stalled against British firepower and fuel shortages (Royal Navy interdiction of Axis Mediterranean supply convoys created chronic German fuel crisis). After five days of attacks, Rommel withdrew September 5—his last offensive in North Africa. The 10th Armoured Division's defensive success validated Montgomery's methodical approach: prepared positions, combined arms, material superiority over tactical maneuver.

## 4. Second Battle of El Alamein (October 23 - November 4, 1942)

### Phase 1: Operation Lightfoot (October 23-24)

The 10th Armoured Division's role during Montgomery's opening assault paralleled 1st Armoured: wait for XXX Corps infantry breakthrough through Axis minefields, then exploit gaps with armoured thrust. Engineers cleared corridors through 500,000+ mines in defensive belts up to 5 miles deep—slow, dangerous work under artillery and machine gun fire.

Division tanks moved forward night of October 23-24 following 51st Highland, 2nd New Zealand, 9th Australian, and 1st South African infantry divisions. However, minefield clearance delays, congestion in breach corridors, and fierce German anti-tank fire slowed advance. Rommel positioned 88mm Flak guns in anti-tank roles with interlocking fields of fire, backed by hull-down Panzer IVs—deadly ambushes for British tanks attempting breakthrough before infantry secured corridors.

### Phase 2: The "Dogfight" (October 25 - November 1)

Eight days of brutal attritional warfare followed as Montgomery's forces ground through Axis defenses. The 10th Armoured Division's 8th and 24th Armoured Brigades engaged 15th and 21st Panzer Divisions in tank-vs-tank combat, gradually depleting German armoured reserves. Sherman and Grant tanks proved more survivable than earlier Crusaders, with heavier armor and better crew escape arrangements, but struggled against up-gunned Panzer IV Ausf. F2/G with long 75mm L/43 at long range.

British artillery superiority proved decisive: division's two field regiments (104th RHA, 147th Essex Yeomanry—48 total 25-pounders) contributed to massive fire concentrations. Eighth Army fired over 1 million 25-pounder rounds during 12-day battle—rate of expenditure impossible for Axis forces. Counter-battery fire suppressed German artillery; defensive fire missions broke up counterattacks before reaching British lines.

### Phase 3: Operation Supercharge and Gatehouse's Hesitation (November 2-4)

Montgomery launched decisive blow November 2: concentrated armoured thrust by 1st and 10th Armoured Divisions through northern Axis defenses. Gatehouse initially resisted commitment, arguing inadequate infantry support would cause excessive tank losses. Montgomery insisted breakthrough must proceed—strategic priority trumped tactical casualties.

The assault validated both perspectives: British tanks suffered heavy losses to 88mm guns and Panzer IVs (10th Armoured likely lost 50-70 tanks destroyed/damaged during breakthrough), but concentrated force achieved penetration. German and Italian forces, exhausted after 11 days continuous combat and critically low on fuel/ammunition, began withdrawal night of November 3-4.

### Pursuit Operations (November 5 - December 1942)

The 10th Armoured Division transitioned to pursuit, advancing westward across Cyrenaica toward Tunisia. Division captured Tobruk (November 13), pushed through Benghazi, pursued Rommel's rearguard across 1,200 miles of desert. However, fuel shortages, extended supply lines (stretching from Alexandria 250km+ and growing daily), poor roads, and skillful German rearguard actions prevented decisive destruction of Axis mobile forces.

Rain beginning November slowed operations—desert turned to mud, bogging vehicles, complicating resupply. Nevertheless, strategic victory was complete: Axis never again threatened Egypt or Suez Canal, Rommel retreated to Tunisia facing enemies on two fronts (British from east after Alamein, Americans/British from west after Operation Torch landings November 8).

## 5. Equipment and Combat Power

### Armoured Fighting Vehicles: 218 Tanks

**M4 Sherman II (72 tanks):** American M4 with 75mm M3 gun, game-changer for British armour. Armor: 51mm frontal hull (sloped), 76mm turret front—adequate against German 50mm, vulnerable to 75mm/88mm. Penetration: 60mm at 1,000 yards with M61 AP—effective against Panzer III and early Panzer IV, struggled against Panzer IV Ausf. F2/G (80mm frontal armor). Reliability, mechanical durability, crew comfort superior to British cruiser tanks. Crew: 5. Weight: 30.3 tons. Speed: 25 mph road, 15 mph cross-country. Range: 120 miles. Distribution: Mixed with Grants in 8th and 24th Armoured Brigades.

**M3 Grant (65 tanks):** M3 Lee Medium Tank with British turret redesign. 75mm M2/M3 in hull sponson (limited traverse), 37mm M5/M6 in small turret. High silhouette (10 feet 3 inches) hindered hull-down positioning but 75mm gun's HE capability valuable for infantry support. Armor: 38-51mm frontal—adequate against Italian tanks, German 50mm. Crew: 6-7. Weight: 27.2 tons. Being phased out as more Shermans arrived but still substantial portion of division tank strength at El Alamein.

**Crusader III (25 tanks):** British cruiser tank with 6-pounder (57mm) upgrade. Thin armor (20-49mm) left vulnerable to German guns. Used reconnaissance and close support, supplementing American tanks. Crew: 3-5. Weight: 19.7 tons. Speed: 27 mph. Mechanical reliability issues—breakdown rate higher than Shermans.

**M3 Stuart "Honey" (42 tanks):** American light tank, 37mm M6 gun. Excellent reliability and speed (36 mph) for reconnaissance. Armor: 13-51mm. Crew: 4. Weight: 12.3 tons. Four per armoured regiment headquarters for reconnaissance sections. Gun ineffective against medium/heavy tanks, adequate for armoured cars and light vehicles.

**Crusader II (14 tanks):** Earlier Crusader variant with 2-pounder (40mm) gun—obsolete by 1942 for anti-tank work. Light reconnaissance role only. Crew: 3-5. Weight: 19.3 tons. Speed: 27 mph. Unreliable mechanically, being replaced.

### Armoured Cars: 96 Total

**Humber Armoured Car (38):** Four-wheel reconnaissance vehicle, 15mm armor, .303 Besa MG or 15mm Besa HMG. Crew: 3. Speed: 45 mph. Reliable for desert reconnaissance and patrol.

**Daimler Armoured Car (32):** Four-wheel with 2-pounder gun, Besa coaxial MG. Armor: 6-16mm. Crew: 3. Speed: 50 mph. Forward reconnaissance, flank security, screening.

**Marmon-Herrington Mk II (26):** South African four-wheel armoured car, .303 Vickers MG or Boys anti-tank rifle. Light armor (6-12mm), fast (50 mph), reliable. Crew: 3-4. Long-range reconnaissance and patrol.

## 6. Supply and Logistics

### Supply Status: Adequate for Offensive Operations

British Eighth Army's logistical superiority was decisive. Supply line from Alexandria (250km) via coastal railway and road networks well-established. Forward dumps pre-positioned near El Alamein before offensive: ammunition stockpiles, fuel, water, rations, spare parts. During 12-day battle, unrestricted artillery expenditure impossible for Axis forces supplied from Tripoli 1,800km west under Royal Navy/RAF interdiction.

**Fuel Reserves:** 7.5 days at operational tempo—sufficient for breakthrough, limited pursuit before resupply.

**Ammunition Reserves:** 14 days—massive stockpiles enabled unrestricted fire missions.

**Water Requirements:** 4.5 liters/man/day × 14,450 personnel = 65,025 liters/day. Met by 95 water tankers plus forward dumps.

**Operational Radius:** 145km from supply dumps before resupply critical.

### Supply Chain and Logistics Strain

Alexandria port → railway/truck convoy → forward dumps → division RASC companies → units. Daily truck runs brought supplies, evacuated casualties and damaged equipment. Logistics strain increased during pursuit: extended supply lines, poor roads, fuel for rapid movement, maintenance from desert wear. However, British logistics capacity vastly exceeded Axis—Rommel's chronic fuel shortages never afflicted Montgomery.

## 7. Weather, Terrain, and Desert Adaptation

**Season (1942-Q4):** October-December—autumn to winter, optimal campaigning. Summer heat subsided, winter rains not yet begun. **Temperature:** 11-26°C (52-79°F)—comfortable days, cool nights. **Daylight:** 11 hours—sufficient for daytime operations, long nights for movement and resupply. **Storm frequency:** ~3 days/month—rare sandstorms (khamsin) halted operations with zero visibility.

**Terrain:** El Alamein coastal plain and rocky desert. Firm ground for armour, unlike soft sand south. Limited vegetation. Rocky ridges provided observation/defensive positions. Extensive minefields (500,000+ mines) required systematic clearance. Featureless terrain required sun compasses, dead reckoning, star navigation.

**Desert Adaptations:** Sand filters (critical—desert dust destroyed unprotected engines), desert camouflage/netting, sun compasses, water purification, fuel economy, recovery vehicles, mobile workshops, spare parts stockpiling. Personnel: wide-brimmed hats, goggles, sunburn prevention, water discipline, medical prevention (dysentery, heat, flies).

## 8. Historical Significance and Legacy

### Strategic Impact

Second El Alamein marked Axis high-water mark in North Africa. Combined with Operation Torch (November 8, 1942), Rommel faced enemies on two fronts with overstretched supply. The 10th Armoured Division's breakthrough participation contributed to strategic victory: Axis withdrawal from Egypt, Libya, eventual Tunisia surrender (May 1943, 250,000 prisoners).

Churchill: "Before Alamein we never had a victory. After Alamein we never had a defeat." While hyperbolic, El Alamein was genuine turning point: British demonstrated capability to defeat Axis in offensive operations using material superiority and methodical combined-arms doctrine.

### Operational Lessons

**Material superiority > tactical excellence:** British not tactically superior but overwhelmed Germans with numbers, firepower, logistics. **Combined arms essential:** Independent tank charges failed—coordination between infantry, tanks, artillery, engineers necessary. **Logistics decisive:** Short British supply lines vs extended Axis supply determined outcome. **New equipment enabled new tactics:** Sherman/Grant tanks gave British competitive capability, enabling offensives previously suicidal with Crusaders.

### Division Legacy

The 10th Armoured continued through Tunisia (1943), then disbanded—personnel redistributed. Legacy: demonstration that methodical operations with material superiority could defeat tactically superior opponents. El Alamein validated Montgomery's cautious approach—controversial but effective given British strengths (logistics, firepower) and weaknesses (tactical doctrine, tank technology until Lend-Lease). The division's mixed American-British tank fleet foreshadowed future: British/Canadian forces predominantly used American tanks (Sherman, Sherman Firefly) through Northwest Europe 1944-1945.
