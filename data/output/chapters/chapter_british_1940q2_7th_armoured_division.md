# 7th Armoured Division

**Nation:** British
**Quarter:** 1940-Q2
**Organization Level:** Division

## 1. Overview

The 7th Armoured Division, destined to become legendary as the "Desert Rats," was formed in Egypt in February 1940 from the Mobile Division (Egypt), itself established in September 1938. During the second quarter of 1940 (April-June), the division represented Britain's sole armored formation in the Middle East theater and the cornerstone of Western Desert Force defensive preparations. As Italy's entry into World War II approached, the 7th Armoured Division maintained a vigilant watch along the Libyan-Egyptian frontier from positions near Mersa Matruh.

Under Major-General Michael O'Moore Creagh's command, the division was tasked with defending Egypt's western approaches through mobile operations across the vast Western Desert. The division's organizational structure reflected innovative British armored doctrine: two armored brigades (4th Heavy and 7th Light) supported by the 7th Support Group, an integrated formation of motorized infantry, artillery, and engineers. This combined-arms approach would prove revolutionary in desert warfare, emphasizing mobility, reconnaissance, and coordinated firepower over static defense.

The quarter witnessed Italy's declaration of war on June 10, 1940, transforming the division's training and preparation into active combat operations. On June 11, elements of the 11th Hussars (Prince Albert's Own) conducted the British Army's first offensive action against Italian forces, crossing into Libya to engage forward Italian positions. These initial border skirmishes inaugurated what would become three years of continuous desert warfare and established the 7th Armoured Division's reputation for aggressive patrolling and mobile tactics.

## 2. Command Structure

### Division Commander

**Major-General Sir Michael O'Moore Creagh, KBE, CB, DSO, MC** assumed command on December 4, 1939.

Previous service: Royal Tank Corps veteran with distinguished WWI service, recipient of the Distinguished Service Order and Military Cross for gallantry in France. Creagh was one of the British Army's foremost armored warfare experts, having commanded tanks at Cambrai (1917) and subsequent operations.

Creagh's command philosophy emphasized mobile defense, aggressive reconnaissance, and exploitation of the tank's mobility advantages. He recognized that the Western Desert's vast distances favored mechanized forces capable of rapid concentration and dispersion. His leadership during the pre-war period focused intensively on desert navigation training, vehicle maintenance discipline, and combined arms coordination. Creagh instilled in the division an offensive spirit despite numerical inferiority, directing his armored car regiments to conduct deep penetration patrols into Libya, gathering intelligence and demonstrating British resolve. His insistence on radio communication proficiency and coordinated movement would prove decisive in upcoming operations. However, Creagh faced significant challenges with chronic equipment shortages, particularly the division's severe deficiency in cruiser tanks and the obsolescence of light tank models against newer Italian armor.

## 10. Combat History

### Formation and Deployment

The 7th Armoured Division's origins trace to September 16, 1938, when the Mobile Division (Egypt) was formed under Major-General Percy Hobart. This pioneering formation represented the British Army's first purpose-built armored division designed for desert warfare. Hobart's innovative training methods and tactical doctrines established the foundation for mobile armored operations across vast distances. On February 16, 1940, the formation was redesignated as the 7th Armoured Division, with Major-General O'Moore Creagh assuming command in December 1939 after Hobart's controversial relief.

The division spent the period from formation through June 1940 in intensive training across the Western Desert, mastering navigation, vehicle maintenance, and combined arms tactics. Units conducted extended exercises simulating deep penetration raids, defensive screens, and mobile concentration of force. The 11th Hussars established their legendary reputation through long-range reconnaissance patrols, sometimes venturing 100+ miles into Libya to gather intelligence on Italian dispositions. This pre-war training period proved invaluable, creating a cohesive formation thoroughly adapted to desert conditions.

Upon Italy's declaration of war (June 10, 1940), the division immediately transitioned from training to combat operations. The division's wartime debut came June 11, 1940, when Lieutenant Colonel John Combe led the 11th Hussars across the Libyan frontier in a reconnaissance-in-force. Near Fort Capuzzo, the armored cars engaged Italian frontier guards, capturing prisoners and withdrawing successfully - Britain's first offensive land action against the Axis. These initial border skirmishes inaugurated three years of continuous desert warfare.

### Major Engagements

**June 11-30, 1940 - Border Patrol Operations**

Following Italy's entry into the war, 7th Armoured Division elements conducted aggressive cross-border raids to establish dominance along the frontier. The 11th Hussars' armored car patrols ranged deep into Italian territory, ambushing convoys, capturing isolated posts, and gathering intelligence. On June 14, the 11th Hussars ambushed an Italian column near Nezuet Ghirba, destroying several vehicles. June 16 saw more extensive actions near Sidi Omar, with British armored cars inflicting casualties and withdrawing before Italian reinforcements arrived. These raids demonstrated British tactical superiority and demoralized Italian frontier garrisons, establishing patterns that would characterize early desert warfare: mobility, surprise, and aggressive patrolling against numerically superior but less mobile opponents.

**June 1940 - Establishment of Frontier Dominance**

Throughout the quarter's final weeks, the division maintained pressure on Italian forces while avoiding decisive engagement. The 4th Armoured Brigade's cruiser tanks conducted demonstrations along the frontier, while the Support Group's motorized infantry established defensive positions covering withdrawal routes. These operations achieved strategic objectives: demonstrating British resolve, gathering intelligence on Italian capabilities, and establishing tactical superiority despite numerical inferiority. By quarter's end, the 7th Armoured Division had established the operational patterns that would characterize Operation Compass five months later.

### Combat Effectiveness Assessment

The division's Q2 1940 performance revealed both exceptional strengths and critical vulnerabilities. Tactically, the 7th Armoured demonstrated superior mobility, initiative, and combined arms coordination. The 11th Hussars' reconnaissance prowess proved invaluable, while the Support Group concept of integrating artillery, infantry, and engineers with armor showed tactical innovation. British wireless communication and navigation capabilities far exceeded Italian equivalents.

However, equipment limitations constrained operational effectiveness. The severe tank shortage (228 actual versus 340 authorized) limited sustained operations. Light tank obsolescence against Italian M11/39 medium tanks created vulnerability, while cruiser tank mechanical reliability issues caused frequent breakdowns. Limited spare parts, extended supply lines, and insufficient transport capacity for sustained mobile operations all emerged as constraining factors.

Overall, the division's Q2 1940 combat debut validated its training and tactical doctrine while highlighting the critical importance of adequate equipment. The professional cadre, aggressive leadership, and thorough desert preparation compensated for numerical and material inferiority against an opponent not yet tested in mobile warfare. These initial successes established the 7th Armoured Division's reputation and provided valuable combat experience for upcoming operations.

## 14. Historical Significance

### Strategic Importance

The 7th Armoured Division's Q2 1940 deployment represented Britain's entire mobile armored reserve in the Middle East theater - a strategic vulnerability that could have proven catastrophic had Italian forces launched immediate offensives. The division's successful defense of Egypt's western approaches despite numerical inferiority (10,000 British versus 215,000+ Italians in Libya) demonstrated the force-multiplying effects of mobility, professionalism, and aggressive tactics. Strategically, the division's presence deterred Italian advances and bought time for British reinforcement, protecting the Suez Canal and Britain's vital Mediterranean-Indian Ocean communications.

The division's Q2 1940 operations established operational patterns that would characterize the entire North African campaign: mobile warfare across vast distances, reliance on superior reconnaissance, exploitation of enemy supply vulnerabilities, and combined arms integration. The Support Group concept pioneered by the 7th Armoured - integrating artillery, motorized infantry, and engineers with armor - became doctrinal standard for British armored divisions. The division's successful transition from training to combat operations validated pre-war British armored doctrine and provided confidence for future offensive operations.

### Operational Lessons

Q2 1940 operations yielded critical lessons that shaped subsequent desert warfare doctrine. The primacy of logistics emerged clearly - fuel, water, and ammunition supply determined operational reach far more than tactical considerations. Navigation proved equally critical; units mastered sun compass techniques and celestial navigation essential for desert operations. Wireless communication demonstrated decisive advantages, enabling coordination across hundreds of miles and rapid concentration of dispersed forces.

Equipment lessons were equally stark. Light tank obsolescence against Italian M11/39 medium tanks necessitated urgent re-equipment with heavier cruiser tanks and development of the A15 Crusader. Cruiser tank mechanical reliability emerged as critical - mobility advantages meant nothing if tanks broke down before contact. The 2-pounder anti-tank gun proved adequate against 1940 Italian armor but lacked high-explosive capability for general support. These lessons informed equipment priorities for 1941 reinforcements.

Tactically, the division validated aggressive reconnaissance, mobile defense, and exploitation of interior lines. The success of small, highly mobile forces (11th Hussars' armored car patrols) against numerically superior but less mobile opponents demonstrated desert warfare's unique characteristics. The division learned to operate with minimal supply, improvise repairs with limited parts, and maintain cohesion across dispersed deployments - skills that would prove invaluable in upcoming operations.

### Legacy

The 7th Armoured Division's "Desert Rats" nickname and jerboa (desert rat) insignia became iconic symbols of British desert warfare prowess. Adopted in late 1940, the symbol reflected the division's adaptation to harsh desert conditions and aggressive, resourceful character. The division went on to fight in every major North African campaign: Operation Compass (December 1940-February 1941), the siege of Tobruk, Operation Crusader, Gazala, and El Alamein. It later participated in the Italian campaign and Northwest Europe 1944-1945, ending the war in Berlin.

Q2 1940 established traditions and esprit de corps that sustained the division through three years of desert warfare. The professional excellence, aggressive patrolling, and combined arms integration pioneered in this period became hallmarks of 7th Armoured Division operations. Veterans of the 1940 frontier battles formed the experienced cadre that trained successive reinforcements, passing down lessons and maintaining tactical proficiency.

Historically, the 7th Armoured Division's Q2 1940 operations demonstrated that well-trained, professionally led mobile forces could successfully defend against numerically superior opponents through superior tactics, mobility, and morale. The division's successful combat debut vindicated British armored doctrine, provided valuable combat experience, and established the foundation for Operation Compass - the first major British land victory of World War II.
